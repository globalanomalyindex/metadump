Mammoth Steak: the show plate

What "mammoth" can mean here. Real mammoth isn't something you can buy. Permafrost carcasses exist, but the record shows dogs and scavengers eating them, not a cooking tradition. One researcher who tried frying old meat got "a strange liquid". The closest real eating comes from Yakutia, where horse and reindeer are the stand-ins. So this recipe does what the name promises in two ways. It is a very large, bone-in, tall cut, carried whole to the table and carved in front of everyone. And it uses a buyable stand-in for the meat, with a plain beef option.

Serves 4 with leftovers for a second meal. I assumed 4 adults because you didn't give a head count. See "Scaling" at the end for other sizes.

Choose the meat (pick one)

  • Bone-in bison rib steak or tomahawk, 2.5 to 3 lb (1.1 to 1.4 kg), 2 to 2.5 inches thick. It is lean and a little sweet, and its flavor reads as wild. Bison ranches ship it frozen across the US.
  • Bone-in beef tomahawk, same size. This is the "mammoth as very large" version. A tomahawk is about 2 inches thick with a long bone, and the bone adds length, not thickness. It is easier to find.
  • Reindeer or elephant. Reindeer fillet and tenderloin ship frozen from Swedish game butchers. They are whole cuts, so they work, but they are smaller and leaner, so cut the oven time. Elephant meat sale in the US needs the permits named in 50 CFR parts 13, 14 and 23, so I wouldn't count on it.

If it's frozen: thaw it in the fridge at 40°F or below. Allow about 24 hours per 4 to 5 lb, so a 3 lb cut needs about 24 hours. Thaw it before you salt it.

Ingredients

  • 1 bone-in cut, 2.5 to 3 lb (1.1 to 1.4 kg)
  • Kosher salt: 1% of the meat's weight (about 11 to 14 g, roughly 1 tablespoon of Diamond Crystal)
  • 1 teaspoon coarsely ground black pepper
  • 3 tablespoons high smoke-point oil (avocado or refined sunflower)
  • 4 tablespoons (60 g) butter
  • 4 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 4 thyme sprigs and 2 rosemary sprigs
  • Flaky salt to finish

Equipment: an instant-read or leave-in probe thermometer (not optional), a wire rack on a sheet pan, a heavy cast-iron or carbon-steel pan big enough for the cut, and a carving board.

Method

  1. Dry-brine (24 hours ahead, or at least 12). Pat the steak very dry. Salt it all over, bone side included, at 1% of its weight. Set it on the rack, uncovered, in the fridge. The salt seasons inward and dries the surface. A dry surface is what lets it brown instead of steaming grey.
  2. Warm up (45 to 60 minutes before cooking). Take it out of the fridge, leave it on the rack, and heat the oven to 250°F (120°C). Pat it dry again and add the pepper. Don't salt again.
  3. Cook slowly to the pull temperature. Put the probe in the thickest part of the meat, away from the bone and fat. Roast at 250°F until the core reads 115°F (46°C) for medium-rare. Expect about 45 to 75 minutes for a 2 to 2.5 inch cut. Go by the probe, not the clock, and check in several spots. A probe near the surface reads hotter than the centre.
  4. Rest briefly. Take the steak out and let it sit for 10 minutes while the pan heats. Pat the surface dry again if it looks damp.
  5. Sear. Heat the pan over medium-high to high heat until the oil shimmers and just starts to smoke. Sear the steak for about 1.5 to 2 minutes a side, turning once. Sear the fat cap and edges too, holding them against the pan with tongs. The crust should be deep brown. In the last 30 seconds add the butter, garlic and herbs, tilt the pan, and spoon the foaming butter over the top. Sear time is short because the centre is already warm. A cold centre under a fast sear is how you get a grey band.
  6. Rest and carry over. Move it to the rack and tent it loosely with foil. The core keeps climbing about 10 to 15°F on a roast-sized cut, so it should settle near 130 to 135°F. Rest it 15 to 20 minutes. Carving straight off the heat lets the juices run onto the board.
  7. Carve at the table. Carry the whole thing out on a board or slate. Cut the meat off the bone in one piece, then slice it against the grain, about 1/2 inch thick. Cut the bone into ribs to gnaw on. Finish with flaky salt and spoon over the pan juices.

Doneness targets (core temperature)

| Doneness | Pull at | Settles at after rest |
|---|---|---|
| Rare | about 110°F | 120 to 125°F |
| Medium-rare | about 115°F | 130 to 135°F |
| Medium | about 125°F | 140 to 145°F |
| Medium-well | about 135°F | 150 to 155°F |

A food-safety note. Published guidance gives 145°F for whole-muscle meat, and medium-rare sits below that. Whether you accept that for a taste choice is yours. If you want to follow the minimum, pull at 125°F and finish at 145°F. Venison guidance from Penn State is stricter, 160°F. If your meat is wild-shot game rather than farmed bison, follow that. Farmed and wild meat are treated differently, so check which one you have. Wash your hands and board after handling the raw meat.

About the "gamey" taste

"Gamey" is a catchall. It points to dark red muscle, lean meat, wild fat and overcooking. In lean game the fat carries most of the wild flavor. For a milder result, keep to medium-rare (overcooking makes game drier and stronger), don't skip the dry-brine, and use the butter and herbs at the end. For more wild flavor, trim less of the fat cap, add a little juniper, and skip the butter baste.

Sides that stretch (pick 2 or 3)

  • Roast potatoes or a tray of roast roots, which soak up the juices
  • Charred greens (broccolini or cavolo nero), for bitterness against the sweet crust
  • Pickled red onions and horseradish cream, to cut the richness
  • Mushrooms cooked in the pan juices
  • Crusty bread for the board

If something goes wrong

  • The core is still under target after the rest. Put the steak back on the rack in a 250°F oven until the probe reads the target, rest it 5 minutes, then sear it briefly in a very hot pan. The probe decides, not time.
  • There's a wide grey band under the crust. It forms during the sear, so it can't be undone. A shorter sear keeps it from getting wider. Next time, use a slower oven and a drier surface. This fix comes from reasoning, not a tested source.
  • The crust won't brown. The surface was wet. Pat it dry and let the pan get hotter next time.

Scaling

Plan on 8 to 12 oz of cooked meat per adult. Cooking loses about 20 to 30% of the weight, so buy about a quarter more raw. Thickness sets the cooking time, not how many people you feed. Two cuts of the same thickness cook in about the time of one, if they fit in the pan.

| Servings | Buy (raw) | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1.5 to 2 lb | one cut |
| 4 | 2.5 to 3 lb | one show cut (this recipe) |
| 8 to 12 | 7.5 to 10 lb | one 8 to 10 lb cut, or three 2.5 to 3 lb cuts |

Salt at 1% of weight whatever the size. For a cut over 4 lb, dry-brine 36 to 48 hours and plan on the oven stage running well over 90 minutes.

Leftovers

Cold slices make a good second meal: on bread with horseradish, in a hash, or thin over greens. Reheat gently, because lean game dries out and turns stronger with heat.
