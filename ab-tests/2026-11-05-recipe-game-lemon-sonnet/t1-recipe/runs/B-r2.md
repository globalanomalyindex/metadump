# Mammoth Steak (bone-in bison, reverse-seared, carved at the table)

**What "mammoth steak" means here.** Nobody has a documented, cooked human meal of woolly mammoth. Permafrost carcasses like the Yuka find kept their meat, but the record is dogs and scavengers eating it, plus one researcher whose fried sample turned to liquid. African elephant isn't a clean stand-in either: in the US, products other than ivory need permits under 50 CFR parts 13, 14 and 23. So this recipe uses the closest thing you can actually buy, **bison**. It is a lean, cold-country grazer that sells online as whole ribeye, strip and tenderloin, often flash-frozen. Mammoth comes through the size and the show: a tall, thick, bone-in cut, carried whole to the table and carved in front of everyone. If you'd rather do the other reading, "mammoth" as plain huge, use a 2 inch beef bone-in ribeye or tomahawk and change nothing else below.

**Serves 4 adults, with a few slices left over** (about 8 oz cooked meat each; the raw weight runs about a quarter higher because cooking loses 20 to 30 percent). For 8 to 12 people, buy two or three cuts of the same thickness. Time follows thickness, not headcount, so the timings don't change.

## Ingredients

- 1 bone-in bison ribeye, 3 to 3.5 lb (1.4 to 1.6 kg), **2 inches (5 cm) thick**, bone left long. A tomahawk-style cut is the show shape. Ask the butcher for thickness as well as weight. If you can only get boneless, 2 inch thick works the same.
- Kosher salt, by weight: **8 g** (about 0.6 percent of the meat). That is roughly 3 tsp Diamond Crystal or 1.5 tsp Morton. Morton packs about 1.7 times more salt per teaspoon, so weigh it if you can.
- Black pepper, coarsely cracked: 1 tsp (add after the oven, see below)
- High smoke-point oil (avocado or refined sunflower): 1 tbsp
- Unsalted butter: 3 tbsp (45 g)
- 4 garlic cloves, smashed
- 4 sprigs thyme (rosemary or a few juniper berries, crushed, also suit game)
- Flaky salt to finish

**Equipment:** an instant-read probe thermometer (this decides doneness, not the clock), a sheet pan with a wire rack, a 12 to 14 inch cast-iron pan, tongs, paper towels, a large board.

## 1. Thaw (only if frozen): 3 days ahead

Thaw in the refrigerator at 40 F (4 C) or below, never on the counter. Allow about 24 hours per 4 to 5 lb, so a 3.5 lb cut needs about 20 hours, and 24 to 36 is a safe margin. A bigger cut needs days. Start the dry brine only once it's fully thawed.

## 2. Dry-brine: 12 to 24 hours ahead

1. Pat the meat very dry with paper towels.
2. Sprinkle the 8 g of salt evenly over every surface, including the sides and around the bone.
3. Set it on the wire rack over the sheet pan, **uncovered**, in the refrigerator. Salt seasons inward and the cold air dries the surface, which is what lets the crust brown instead of steam.
4. Lead time grows with thickness. 12 hours is the minimum at 2 inches, and 24 hours is better. Don't put pepper on yet, since it scorches in the sear.

## 3. Bring to the oven

Take it out of the fridge 30 to 45 minutes before cooking. Heat the oven to **225 F (110 C)**, rack in the middle. Pat the surface dry again if any beads of moisture have formed.

## 4. Slow oven phase

1. Leave the meat on its rack and pan. Insert the probe into the thickest part of the meat, away from the bone.
2. Roast until the core reads **115 F (46 C)** for a medium-rare finish. No source I trust gives a clock time for a 2 inch bone-in cut, so my estimate is 45 to 75 minutes. Begin checking at 40 minutes and let the probe decide.
3. The pull temperature is below the final target on purpose. The outside is hotter than the centre, and the centre keeps climbing after the cut leaves the heat (5 to 10 F on a steak this size).

| Finish you want | Pull from the oven at | Final core after sear and rest |
|---|---|---|
| Rare | 108 to 110 F | 120 to 125 F |
| **Medium-rare (recommended)** | **112 to 118 F** | **128 to 135 F** |
| Medium | 125 to 130 F | 140 to 145 F |

Bison is very lean and has little fat to protect it, so I wouldn't cook it past medium. Past that it goes dry and tight.

## 5. Sear

1. Heat the cast-iron pan over high heat until the oil you add shimmers and just starts to smoke.
2. Rub the oil over the meat, then crack the pepper on.
3. Sear **45 to 60 seconds per side**, then 15 seconds on each edge, holding the cut with tongs. Because the oven has already warmed the centre, the sear only has to brown. A long sear on a thick cut just widens the grey band under the crust.
4. In the last 30 seconds, turn the heat to medium, add the butter, garlic and thyme, tilt the pan, and spoon the foaming butter over the top for 20 to 30 seconds.

## 6. Rest

Move the steak to the board (or back on the rack). Rest it **10 to 15 minutes**, loosely tented with foil. Carving straight off the heat lets the juice run out onto the board instead of staying in the meat. The core should climb 5 to 10 F; check it once more and write the number down if you're a nerd.

## 7. Carve at the table

Carry the steak out whole on the board. Cut the meat away from the bone in one piece, then slice it **across the grain**, about 1/4 to 1/2 inch thick (6 to 12 mm). You'll see the full cross-section from crust to centre. Fan the slices on a warm platter, spoon the pan butter over, scatter flaky salt, and put the bone on the board as the centrepiece. Gnawing on it is the traditional use.

## Food safety note

Sources disagree on the doneness number for game. Under the Washington state rule, intact whole-muscle meat, including commercially raised game, reaches 145 F, held 15 seconds. Penn State's venison guidance says 160 F. Medium-rare (130 to 135 F) is below both, which many cooks accept as a taste choice, but you should know you're choosing. If you want to follow the published minimum, pull at 125 to 130 F and finish to 145 F, and treat the steak as medium. Pregnant or immunocompromised guests, or anyone cautious, should get the 145 F version.

## If it goes wrong

- **The core is still low after the rest** (more than 5 F under your target): put it back on the rack in a 225 to 275 F oven until the probe reads your target minus about 5 F, rest 5 minutes, then give each side 20 to 30 seconds in the smoking pan.
- **A wide grey band shows under the crust:** it formed during browning, and I haven't found a source that fixes it after the fact. A short sear won't make it worse. Next time, sear for less time and finish more in the oven.
- **Grey crust with a cool centre:** the surface was damp or the centre started cold. Dry it harder, warm it longer in the oven.

## Make it gamier or milder

Bison is milder than most game, but the flavour lives in the fat and the surface. For a cleaner flavour, trim silver skin and loose fat, keep the salt lead long, and baste with butter. A 2 to 12 hour milk soak works too, but you must then rinse, dry, and still salt. Acid marinades (red wine, juniper, vinegar) work on the surface only, 1 to 4 hours, and a long soak softens it. For gamey on purpose, salt only, pepper, and nothing else.

## What goes with it

Roasted root vegetables (the ice-age plate: parsnip, carrot, turnip), a pan of charred onions, or a sharp mustard. Leftover slices are good cold the next day.

## Where to buy

- **Bison:** US ranches ship whole ribeye, strip and tenderloin nationwide, usually frozen. A Canadian retailer sells flash-frozen packs. Ground bison is the easiest first buy if you're just testing, but this recipe needs a whole thick cut.
- **Reindeer (swapping for a different Ice Age animal):** a Swedish online game butcher sells frozen fillet (renfilé). It's much smaller and thinner than a ribeye, so cut the oven time to the probe, not the numbers above.
- **Horse** is an everyday protein in Yakutia and is the third choice here, but it isn't easy to find in most places.
