# Field Guide to the Fauna of the High Country

## *Draco montanus* — The Cinder-Backed Dragon

**Also called:** ember wyrm, the mountain's grandmother, "the long weather"
**Family:** Dracontidae (the great winged reptiles)
**Conservation status:** Vulnerable. Fewer than three hundred breeding adults are believed to survive.

---

### Identification

**Size.** Adults measure 18 to 25 metres from snout to tail-tip, with a wingspan roughly 1.4 times body length. Females run larger. Hatchlings are about the size of a large hound.

**Colouring.** The back is slate-grey with a rust-orange mottling along the spine, the "cinder" that gives the species its name. The pattern fades to dull bronze on the flanks and to pale cream on the belly. Old individuals darken and take on a lichen-like crust of green and ochre, so a very old dragon can pass for a rock outcrop.

**Key field marks.**
- Two swept-back horns, ridged like ram's horns, with a third, smaller horn at the nose.
- Wing membranes that look translucent amber when backlit.
- Vertical-slit pupils in a gold iris. The eyes shine bright orange in torchlight.
- A faint heat shimmer in the air above the shoulders, even on cold mornings.

**Compare with** the **Marsh Drake** (*Draco paludis*), which is smaller, wingless-looking when folded, and always near water. It is green-brown and has no horns.

### Voice and Sign

- **Call:** A low, resonant hum felt in the chest before it is heard, usually at dusk. A rising rumble means an alarmed animal. If you hear that, retreat.
- **Tracks:** Three-toed prints up to 60 cm long, with deep claw gouges. Tracks usually stop abruptly where the animal took off.
- **Scorch marks:** Blackened, glassy patches on rock faces. These mark territorial boundaries and old kill sites.
- **Scat:** Dense, grey, ash-like pellets containing bone fragments, horn, and the occasional metal buckle.
- **Shed scales:** Palm-sized, curved, warm to the touch for hours after dropping. Collecting them is illegal in most districts.

### Habitat and Range

The Cinder-Backed Dragon lives on high, volcanic or granite massifs above the treeline, between 1,800 and 3,500 metres. It favours south-facing caves and cliff ledges that catch afternoon sun and offer a clear drop for launching. Warm vents and hot springs make a site especially desirable. Individuals hold huge home ranges of 400 to 900 square kilometres, and in winter some descend to forested valleys.

### Behaviour

**Solitary and territorial.** Adults live alone except during courtship. Dragons seldom fight. Territorial disputes are usually settled by flyovers, displays of flame, and a lot of loud humming.

**Diet.** Mostly large ungulates: elk, wild cattle, and mountain goats. They will also take livestock when wild prey is scarce, which is the root of most conflict with people. A dragon can fast for months after a large meal.

**Flight.** Dragons ride thermals and mountain updrafts and flap rarely. In the right conditions a dragon can glide for hours, and they are most often seen soaring over ridgelines in mid-morning, once the rock has warmed.

**Fire.** The "breath" is a pressurised jet of ignited vapour from a gland at the base of the throat. It has a range of 15 to 30 metres and needs a recovery period of several minutes. Dragons use fire mainly to flush prey, to warm eggs, and to threaten. They seldom use it on a creature that has not provoked them.

**Hoarding.** Dragons gather bright, reflective, or warm-feeling objects, such as coins, polished metal, and glass. The reason is debated. Current thinking is that the hoard serves as a courtship display, with larger and shinier hoards attracting mates.

### Breeding

Courtship takes place in late spring, with spiralling aerial displays over a high peak. A clutch of two to four eggs is laid in a hot-spring nest and tended for about eleven months. Eggs are leathery, grey-green, and about the size of a barrel. Young stay with the mother for three years. Dragons are thought to live 200 years or more.

### Where and When to See One

- **Best season:** Late spring through early autumn.
- **Best time of day:** Mid-morning, when updrafts begin, and the last hour before dusk.
- **Best approach:** Scan high ridgelines from a valley floor with good binoculars (10x42 or better). Look for a slow-moving "boulder" that changes position or a shadow with wings.
- **Good vantage points:** Windswept saddles, exposed scree slopes, and above hot springs.

### Safety and Conduct

1. **Keep your distance.** Stay at least 300 metres from any dragon, and never approach a nest.
2. **Do not feed or bait.**
3. **Avoid shiny gear.** Cameras with bright lenses and metallic flasks can draw curiosity.
4. **If one approaches,** stay still, lower your gaze, and back away slowly. Do not run, as running triggers the chase response.
5. **Never take scales, eggs, or hoard items.** A dragon remembers a thief for decades.

### Field Notes

*"I'd watched the ridge for two hours before I understood that the grey mass on the far shelf was breathing."* — typical first-sighting account

---

**Similar species:** Marsh Drake (*D. paludis*), Frost Wyrm (*D. glacialis*), Lesser Hearth-Lizard (*Pyrosaura minor*), the last of which is often mistaken for a dragon hatchling and is harmless.
