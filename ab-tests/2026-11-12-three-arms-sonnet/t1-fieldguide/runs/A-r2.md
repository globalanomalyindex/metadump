# Field Guide: The Cinderback Dragon (*Pyrodraco cinereus*)

**Also called:** ash wyrm, hearth-eater, the Quiet Fire
**Family:** Draconidae, subfamily Embervorinae
**Conservation status:** Near Threatened. Fewer than 400 adults are thought to survive.

---

## Identification

The Cinderback is a mid-sized, heavily built dragon, easy to mistake at a distance for a smoking boulder. Its defining feature is the **ember seam**: a double row of thin, glowing cracks that runs along the spine from the skull to the base of the tail. In calm air the seam glows a dull orange, like a banked coal. When the animal is alarmed, feeding or displaying, it brightens to a clear yellow-white.

**Key field marks**

- **Hide:** matte charcoal grey, with a pale ash-colored bloom on older animals. The scales are broad, overlapping plates with a rough, pumice-like finish. They do not shine.
- **Ember seam:** the dorsal glow described above. It is the surest mark, and it is visible at night from several kilometers.
- **Head:** broad and wedge-shaped, with a heavy brow ridge and two short, swept-back horns. Eyes are a deep amber with a vertical pupil that narrows to a thread in daylight.
- **Wings:** leathery and dark, with a fine mesh of pale veins. In flight the wing membranes show a faint heat shimmer along the trailing edge.
- **Underside:** paler and smoother, a dusty grey-brown. The belly plates are thinner than the dorsal plates, so adults avoid showing them in a fight.
- **Tail:** thick at the base and tapering, ending in a blunt, club-like knot of fused scales.

**Sexes:** Males are larger and have a brighter, wider ember seam. Females have a narrower seam and a slightly longer tail relative to body length. Juveniles lack the seam entirely until about age 30, when the first cracks begin to glow.

## Habitat and Range

Cinderbacks favor places where the ground itself is warm: calderas, fumarole fields, hot springs, and old lava flows. The warmth matters more than the terrain. A Cinderback needs a stable heat source to rest against, which allows it to conserve the energy it would otherwise spend keeping its core temperature up.

Each animal holds a territory of roughly 200 to 400 km². The core of the territory is a **den**, usually a deep cave or collapsed lava tube with a thermal vent. Dens are used for decades, and a good one may be passed from a parent to its young.

After a forest fire the species sometimes ranges outside its usual altitude to hunt the displaced and weakened animals. It tends to return to the heights within a few weeks.

---

## Behavior

**Hunting.** The Cinderback is an ambush hunter that uses its resemblance to rock. It will lie still on a ridge for hours, then drop on prey from above. Its breath weapon, a short, focused jet of superheated gas, is used mainly to flush prey from cover and to stun it, not to cook it. Adults are careful with it, since each discharge draws on stored energy that takes many hours to rebuild.

**Breath.** The breath is not a continuous flame. It is a pressurized burst lasting one to two seconds, with a range of about 30 m. A Cinderback that has just breathed is noticeably weakened: the ember seam dims, and the animal is slower and less willing to fight. This is the best-documented window of vulnerability.

**Display.** In a threat display the animal rears up, spreads its wings, and flares the seam to full brightness while producing a deep, resonant hum that can be felt in the chest before it is heard. Most confrontations end here. Actual fights are rare and brief.

**Social structure.** Solitary, apart from a mother and her young. Neighboring adults avoid one another and mark territory boundaries with scorch marks on rock faces and with scent from glands at the base of the tail. Males and females meet briefly in spring.

**Sounds.** A low hum (display and contact), a dry crackling hiss (alarm), and a rolling rumble (distant calling between adults). The sounds are low enough that they carry well through rock.

---

## Diet

Large grazers and browsers make up most of the diet: wild cattle, elk, mountain goats, and wild boar. A single large kill can sustain an adult for two to three weeks, and the animal will often sleep through the digestion, motionless and with the seam burning low.

Cinderbacks also take fish from hot-spring pools, scavenge carcasses, and occasionally raid herds of domestic livestock at the edge of their range. Livestock losses are the main source of conflict with people.

---

## Life Cycle

- **Courtship:** spring, at the edge of one adult's territory, with mutual displays of seam brightness.
- **Eggs:** a clutch of two to four, laid in a heated chamber in the den. Eggs are dark, roughly the size of a barrel, and warm to the touch. They are incubated by geothermal heat, and the mother stays nearby for about 14 months.
- **Hatchlings:** about 1.2 m long, dull grey, with no seam. They stay in the den for two years and learn to fly at around age 3.
- **Juveniles:** disperse at 8 to 12 years and wander widely until they can claim a territory.
- **Maturity:** reached around age 40, when the seam is fully developed.

---

## Observing Safely

- **Keep your distance.** At least 500 m is recommended, and further when the animal is feeding or has young.
- **Watch the seam.** A rapidly brightening seam means the animal has noticed you. A dimming seam after a breath means it is tired but still dangerous.
- **Do not approach a den.** Mothers with eggs are the most defensive animals of the species.
- **Mind the wind.** Cinderbacks have a good sense of smell. Approach from downwind.
- **Avoid dusk and dawn** when the animals are most active.
- **Never use fire or bright lights**, which are read as challenges.

---

## Notes and Lore

Highland communities have long regarded the Cinderback as a bad-weather omen when it flies low and as a sign of good fortune when its seam is seen glowing on a hillside at night. Old trappers say that a den with fresh scorch marks at the entrance is occupied and one without is not. This is generally reliable.
