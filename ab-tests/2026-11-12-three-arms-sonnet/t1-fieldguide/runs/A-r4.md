# Field Guide Entry: The Cinderback Wyrm (*Draco cinereus*)

**Common names:** Cinderback, ash-wyrm, hearth-drake
**Family:** Draconidae (the true dragons)
**Conservation status:** Near Threatened. Fewer than 400 mature individuals are thought to remain.

---

## At a Glance

| | |
|---|---|
| **Length** | 9 to 14 m, nose to tail tip |
| **Wingspan** | 16 to 22 m |
| **Weight** | 2.5 to 4 tonnes (hollow-boned, so lighter than it looks) |
| **Lifespan** | 200 to 300 years, possibly more |
| **Active** | Dusk and dawn; torpid in midday heat |
| **Voice** | A low, rolling thrum, felt in the chest more than heard |

## Identification

The Cinderback is easiest to recognize by its **dorsal plates**. They are charcoal-grey and flat-topped, and they glow dull orange along the seams when the animal is warm or agitated. A resting adult looks like a ridge of cooling slag.

**Key field marks**

1. **Ash-grey hide**, paler on the throat and belly (a dusty cream), darkening to near-black on the spine.
2. **Ember seams**: thin lines of warm color between the plates. They are brightest after feeding or flight.
3. **Swept-back horns**: two pairs, the rear pair longer. Older males may have a third, shorter pair.
4. **Amber eyes** with a vertical, slit pupil that narrows to a thread in daylight.
5. **Membrane wings** with dark finger-bones and translucent panels. In backlight the panels glow rust-red.

**Sex differences:** Females are larger and usually have a smoother, less jagged crest. Males carry a throat sac that inflates during display.

## Similar Species

| Species | How to tell it apart |
|---|---|
| **Frostmaw Wyrm** | Pale blue-white; lives above the snowline; no ember glow |
| **Greenscale Drake** | Smaller (under 6 m); forest dweller; has no horns |
| **Common Wyvern** | Only two limbs, with wings serving as forelegs; a barbed tail |

The wyvern is the most frequent misidentification. Count the legs: a Cinderback has **four limbs plus wings**, while a wyvern has **two legs plus wings**.

## Habitat and Range

The Cinderback lives on **volcanic uplands, lava fields and geothermal basins**. It favors cave systems near active vents, where the rock stays warm through winter. Its range runs in a loose arc across the high ridgelines of the northern mountains. It is usually found between 1,200 and 3,000 m.

Individuals may roam up to 80 km from the lair in search of food, but they return to the same nest site for decades.

## Behavior

- **Thermoregulation.** The Cinderback basks on sun-warmed basalt or vent-heated stone and is sluggish until its core temperature rises. A cold dragon is a slow dragon. This is the best time to observe one, from a respectful distance.
- **Flight.** It climbs on thermals, then glides for long distances with few wingbeats. Takeoff takes a running leap and a heavy downstroke, and it is noisy.
- **Fire.** The "fire" is a burning vapor produced by two glandular secretions that ignite on contact with air. Range is about 15 to 25 m. Dragons use it sparingly because the glands take hours to recharge.
- **Hoarding.** Cinderbacks gather shiny objects, especially metal and polished stone. The leading explanation is that the objects act as heat reservoirs and nest insulation. Display value to mates is also likely.
- **Social structure.** Solitary, apart from a mother and her young. Territories are marked with scorched rock and claw gouges on cave walls.

## Diet

An apex predator and opportunist. Typical prey includes **mountain goats, wild boar, large elk and (occasionally) livestock**. A Cinderback may feed heavily and then fast for weeks. It will also scavenge carrion.

## Breeding

Females lay a clutch of **2 to 4 eggs** in a heated nest chamber, usually every 15 to 20 years. The eggs are leathery and grey with a faint ember-colored mottling. Incubation takes about **14 months**, and the mother stays close throughout. Hatchlings are about the size of a large dog and cannot fly or breathe fire for their first two years.

## Where and When to Observe

- **Best season:** Late autumn, when cooling nights drive dragons to bask at dawn.
- **Best time of day:** The hour after sunrise.
- **Best method:** Use a spotting scope from a ridge **at least 1 km away and downwind**. Look for rising heat shimmer, scorched patches and wide, shallow scrapes in the rock.
- **Signs:** Scorched ground, shed scales (palm-sized, charcoal, warm-looking even when cold), large droppings with bone fragments, and a faint smell of struck flint.

## Field Safety

- Never approach a nest or lair. A mother with eggs is extremely dangerous.
- Do not feed or leave out bait.
- Keep your distance, stay downwind and do not use flash or bright lights.
- If a dragon turns to face you, its throat begins to glow, or its crest flattens, retreat slowly and out of its line of sight.

## Notes for Your Journal

Record the date, time, location, estimated size, behavior and the intensity of the ember glow. The glow is a quick way to judge how warm and how alert the animal is. Sketch the horn pattern if you can, since it varies between individuals and helps with tracking.
