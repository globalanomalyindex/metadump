## Ashfall Wyrm
**Draco cinereus** (Latin name used once; elsewhere "the wyrm"). Also: cinder-drake, grey lindworm (local, north valleys).

*Field guide entry. Single species page. Plate 14.*

---

### First, the trace

You will probably meet this animal as a line of burnt grass, not as an animal. Look for a scorched strip 30-40 cm wide and 6 to 15 m long, blackest at the downhill end, running along the edge of a game trail. Beside it there is often a shed skin: grey, translucent, in pieces no longer than a hand, with a faint diamond tiling. Fresh skin is pale and flexes. Skin more than a week old goes brittle and smells of struck flint.

The burn line is not a fire the animal set while hunting. It is residue from display (see Defence). Fresh burn lines smell of hot iron for about a day. Old ones look like ordinary lightning scars, but lightning scars branch and these do not.

Work back from the trace to the animal, and check the place first, because range narrows the candidates before the body does.

### Range and habitat

- **Range:** the north-facing scree and fir-line of two ridge systems, between roughly 1,400 and 2,100 m. Not recorded on south aspects or above the last dwarf birch.
- **Season:** present April to October. Records stop abruptly in the first week of November and resume the following April. Where it spends the winter is not known (see Notes).
- **Habitat:** open slope above forest where carrion collects: avalanche runouts, cliff bases below goat ledges, dry streambeds. It drinks at seeps, not at open water.
- **Out-of-range records:** two sightings below 900 m (both in July, both unconfirmed) should be treated as vagrants or misidentified lizards.

### Identification

**Size.** Adult snout-to-tail length 5 to 7 m, about the length of a large Komodo dragon doubled and a little more. Body mass is not reliably known. Estimates from trackway depth run 180 to 300 kg. Wingspan, when the sail is fully spread, is roughly 1.5 times body length.

**Shape.** Long, low and four-legged. The head is narrow, about as long as a man's forearm and a half. The neck is long enough that the head can be carried above the shoulder when the animal stops to look.

**The wing is not a bird or bat wing.** It is a membrane stretched on six elongated ribs (five to seven in the flying lizards it resembles), with the forelimbs joining its leading edge. From below the spread wing shows as a fan with ribs radiating like a hand-held screen. Folded, it lies along the flank as a loose flap of skin. The animal glides between slope and slope. It does not flap, and it cannot climb off flat ground.

**Marks, in the order you can see them from a distance:**

1. A dull grey back, blotched with slate, that breaks up the outline against scree. At 100 m, shape is the field mark, not colour.
2. A **pale underside** that shows as the animal crosses overhead.
3. A **throat flash** of rust-orange, normally hidden, shown only in display.
4. A **blackened jaw** in older animals: the scales around the mouth are stained dark with residue from the display described below. A pale-jawed animal is young.
5. Forked tongue, flicked continuously while walking.

### Lookalikes

| Species | Separating mark |
|---|---|
| Large monitor lizard | No membrane. Heavy tail held low, no sail. Comes to carrion by smell and does not glide. |
| Rib-membrane glider (small, tree-living) | Under 40 cm, always in canopy. If it is on scree it is not this. |
| Two-legged "wyvern" form (reported, not confirmed in this range) | Count the legs. The wing takes the place of the forelimbs. Our animal has four legs plus wing ribs. |

### Diet and hunting

Mainly carrion, found by scent. The forked tongue samples the air, and the animal turns toward a carcass from a long way off (the Komodo, its closest living comparison, is documented tracking carrion at around 4 km). It also ambushes at trail bends, waiting motionless for hours. Feeding is occasional and large. In the Komodo, a single meal can reach about 80 percent of body weight, so the animal is then heavy, slow and fasts afterwards. The same is likely here. A fresh kill with the carcass opened from the belly is therefore a good clue that a wyrm is within a day's walk, and probably full and sluggish.

### Activity

Dawn and late afternoon. It basks on dark rock in the morning, glides to feed in the afternoon, and is torpid at night, when the burn lines are never laid. Do not look for it in rain.

### Defence and display

This animal bluffs before it bites.

1. **Display.** The wings and throat flash spread together, which makes the animal appear several times larger for a moment. A thin oil from glands under the jaw ignites in air and burns for a few seconds, which is what scorches the grass. It is a display and not a weapon. The flame reaches about a metre.
2. **Escape.** If the bluff fails, the animal runs upright for a short stretch on its hind legs, as the frilled lizard does, then drops from a ledge and glides. A frightened animal may disgorge its last meal on the way down to lighten itself.
3. **Bite.** Used only when cornered. The teeth are serrated.

Do not approach to within the length of the animal. Do not stand downhill of a ledge.

### Lifecycle

Clutches of 8 to 14 eggs are buried in warm scree on south-facing pockets just below the range (the only time the species is found on a south aspect). The hatchlings are 40 cm and pale-jawed, and they stay in the nest area through the first summer. Age at maturity is not known. The blackened jaw is the best age mark available, and the oldest animals seen have jaws black to the eye.

### Traces and sightings

| Sign | What it tells you |
|---|---|
| Burn line, one-day-old, hot-iron smell | Animal displayed within 24 hours, nearby |
| Shed skin, diamond tiling | Present in this season, probably an adult |
| Three-toed track, 25 cm, with a drag line | Adult, walking, tail low |
| Opened carcass, unburnt | Feeding, not display |

A sighting line should give the place, the date and the time. Wyrms are seen early and late and not at midday, so a midday record is more likely to be something else.

### Notes and sources

- **Winter range unknown.** No record, no nest, no shed skin has been found between November and April. Either the animal descends further than anyone has surveyed or it holds up in rock. This is the next question for a surveyor.
- **Disputed:** the flame. Two observers who watched at close range say the oil does not ignite by itself and that the animal strikes its teeth on rock. Neither claim has been tested. The burn lines are not disputed.
- The wing description draws on published work on flying lizards of the genus *Draco* (patagium on five to seven elongated ribs, forelimbs on the leading edge). The scent and meal figures are from the Smithsonian National Zoo's account of the Komodo dragon. Everything about the Ashfall Wyrm itself is the guide's own, as is the sighting data.
