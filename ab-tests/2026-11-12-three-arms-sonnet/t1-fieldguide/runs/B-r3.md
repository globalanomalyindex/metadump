# Ember-throated Wyrm
***Draco cinereus* (Aldous, 1411), the ash wyrm, also "cinder-neck" and "the Skerry lizard"**

*Scope: one species, the nominate form. Two island races are noted at the end but not described.*

**Name.** *Draco* is the old genus. *Cinereus* means ash-coloured and was given for the pale, ash-like dust that covers a freshly sloughed adult, not for the living colour (see Colour). First record: the sheep-reeve's roll of Hallow Skerry, which counts nine ewes "burned along one line" and names no animal. Aldous, who described the species, never saw a live one. Take the name as a record of the traces, not of an animal he handled.

**Identification.** A four-legged, long-tailed reptile with a low body, held close to the ground. Wings are absent. The forelimbs are heavy and the hindlimbs a little longer, so it rises onto its hind legs to run.

- *Throat:* a loose dewlap from chin to breastbone. It is dull grey-brown at rest. When the animal is alarmed it flushes orange-red along the lower edge only. This is the surest mark at a distance.
- *Tail:* five to seven pale bands on the last third, narrowing toward the tip. Older animals lose the outer bands.
- *Jaw:* in adults over about twenty years the scales along the lower jaw are black and glossy, as if smoke-stained. This comes from the oil of the gular glands (see Behaviour). Young animals lack it.
- *Feet:* four claws on each forefoot, never five. Under the old court rule of five claws for the emperor and four for lesser dragons, this is a lesser dragon in the strict sense, and every Skerry reeve who has measured the point agrees.
- *Underside:* pale, nearly white, and the only part that is clean after rain.

**Colour.** Disputed, and reported here as seen. The usual report is dull slate over a pale belly, which breaks the outline against scree. Two witnesses (Hild Ormesdottir, 1388; the anonymous "R." of the 1412 supplement) report a sheen like oil on water along the shoulders in strong sun. I have not seen it. It may be real, or the shed dust catching light.

**Size.** Snout to tail-tip, 3.4 m is the figure most often given. That is a little over the longest verified Komodo dragon (3.13 m, 166 kg) and about the length of a small boat. By weight it is probably nearer 150 kg than 300, because the body is narrow. The 6 m "Cragmouth specimen" in the sheep-reeve's annex is attested only by a hide that has since been lost and should not be used. The wing-less body is the reason: a 6 m animal with this build would need a mass the island's scavenging cannot feed (see Diet). The dewlap, fully opened, is about the width of the head again on each side, much as the frill of the frilled lizard (30.5 cm across) doubles the apparent head of that small animal. Observers who give a "great size" were looking at the open dewlap.

**Range and season.** Hallow Skerry, the Rook Stacks and the south shore of Brannock Sound, below about 400 m, on south- and west-facing screes with a gull colony within a day's walk. Seen from April to early October. In winter animals are in dens, probably the same ones, and no one has recorded a winter sighting. A record from the mainland (Dunmar, 1403) is a vagrant at best and is probably a large monitor taken from a ship.

**Habitat.** Bare scree with crevices deep enough to keep out frost; pools of rainwater on rock; a sea-bird cliff close enough to feed on. It avoids bog and turf, which do not hold heat.

**Diet.** Mostly carrion, mainly sea-bird, seal and drowned sheep. Smell leads the hunt: the forked tongue samples the air, and the animal walks with an undulating gait toward a kill it may be several kilometres from. (The Komodo, our nearest analog, is credited with finding carrion at 4 km.) It also waits on game-trails for hours. A single large meal may be most of a quarter of its body weight, followed by weeks without food. A gorged animal is heavy and slow, and the bulge in the flank is visible.

**Behaviour.** Solitary, except that two or three may share a warm ridge in early spring. The dewlap is a bluff: the animal gapes, opens the dewlap and holds still. If that fails it runs upright toward the water, and a fleeing animal may bring up its last meal to lighten itself. It bites only when cornered. Bites are deep and slow to heal.

The *fire* in the folk name is narrower than the tales claim. During display the gular glands release an oil that ignites in air for a moment, a line of flame about the length of an arm. It does not carry. It is what burned the nine ewes: they were lying in the line of an animal driving off a rival from the carcass. It blackens the jaw scales of older animals, which is why the jaw is a better age mark than size.

**Hoard.** The word is borrowed. Roosts hold mixed things for mixed causes: bones, shed scale, a few bright stones, and in two cases coins, which the animal presumably took from drowned men. No one has seen it gather the coins on purpose.

**Life cycle.** Eggs are laid in a pit on a south slope in June, and the female stays near them. The clutch is reported as twelve to twenty-one eggs by the reeves and as thirty by Ormesdottir. The figures have not been reconciled, and the record gives no reason to prefer one. Young are about 40 cm at hatching, pale, with no dewlap colour and no bands. They first show the orange flush in their second year. The lifespan in the wild is thought to be near thirty years from the jaw-blackening of the oldest known animal, a figure I regard as sound only to within ten years.

**Traces.** Three-toed-looking tracks that are really four claws with the inner one dragging; a drag-mark of the tail between them. Shed skin in grey sheets, often caught in crevices. Scorched grass in a single line, usually under a metre wide. Scat full of feather and white with lime. Fresh tracks next to a fresh kill are the best sign that one is within a few hours.

**Lookalikes.**
- *Large monitor lizard* (ship-carried, not native): no dewlap colour and no banded tail; the tongue is the same, so do not rely on it.
- *Gliding lizard, genus* Draco *in the older sense*: a rib-supported membrane (five to seven ribs) at the flank. This wyrm has no membrane at all. If the animal has one, it is a different animal.
- *Wyvern of the northern coast:* two legs with wings. Count the legs.

**What to watch for.** Never approach between the animal and the water; it runs upright and will not turn from the shore. Do not walk a scorched line after dusk. A gorged animal is slow but not tame, and the dewlap flush means it has seen you.

**Notes, and what is not known.**
1. The size is disputed (see above), and the 3.4 m figure is a median of four witnesses who did not measure.
2. The winter dens have not been found. It may be that the animal moves, not hibernates.
3. Whether the sheen on the shoulders exists is not settled.
4. I have not fixed the clutch size. A second source on this point would be welcome from anyone who goes out to the Stacks next June.

*Sources:* sheep-reeve's roll, Hallow Skerry (c. 1411); Aldous, *On the Lesser Dragons* (1411); H. Ormesdottir's letters (1388); "R.", supplement (1412). Analog figures for the Komodo dragon and frilled lizard are from the Smithsonian's National Zoo and the Denver Zoo.

*Recorded at Brannock, third week of September. Gaps as stated.*
