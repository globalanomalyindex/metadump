# Fjord Wyrm

**Draco fafnirensis** · also *lindworm* (Norse), *the long cold one* (sealers' name)

*Scope: one species, the fjord wyrm, as recorded along the north-west coast of the survey area. Other dragon kinds are not covered here.*

---

## Signs to look for first

You will usually meet this animal before you meet its body. Check the ground.

- **Shed skin.** Pale grey-brown sheets, 2 to 4 m long, usually in one piece, caught on the rocks above the tide line. Each scale is about the size of a thumbnail, and the skin is torn open along the belly, never the back. Fresh sheds are damp and smell of kelp. Old ones go brittle and chalky.
- **Scorched line.** A strip of killed grass 6 to 10 m long and one stride wide, with a burnt centre and a brown, curled edge. The soil under it is dry to a depth of about a hand. It runs downhill, in the direction the animal was facing.
- **Track.** Four-toed, with the toes spread wide and the long middle claw cutting a groove. Between the prints, a smooth dragged furrow 15 to 20 cm across where the tail trails. A belly drag with no foot marks beside it means a very young animal or a very tired one.
- **Call.** A low, two-note rumble at dusk, the second note lower, carrying about a kilometre over still water. The sealers say it sounds like a boat hull dragged over shingle.

If you find all four in one cove, the animal is probably within two days' travel.

## Field marks

1. **Banded tail.** Five to seven dark bands on the rear half of the tail, each about a hand-span apart, on a grey-green base. This is the best mark at a distance, and it is the one to look for when the animal is on a far shingle bank.
2. **Pale underside.** The belly and the inside of the throat are cream to bone, sharply set off from a dark back. The line between the two runs along the flank and shows when the animal turns.
3. **Wings: folded, never raised on the ground.** Skin over five long finger-bones, held tight along the back like a folded umbrella. Wingless individuals are reported (see Notes), so a missing wing alone does not rule the species out.
4. **Throat flash.** A patch of rust-orange skin under the jaw, shown only when the animal is alarmed. At rest it is hidden in a fold.
5. **Claws.** Four toes on each foot. The middle claw is the longest, about the length of a man's forearm in a full adult.

## Size

| | Measure | Set beside |
|---|---|---|
| Length, snout to tail tip | 9 to 12 m | a city bus, or roughly twice a large saltwater crocodile |
| Head | 1.1 m long | a rowing-boat oar |
| Wingspan | 14 to 16 m | about 1.4 times the body length |
| Throat flash, spread | 0.8 m across | a bin lid |
| Weight (estimated, not weighed) | 1.5 to 2 tonnes | a pair of adult walrus |
| Hatchling | 0.7 m, under 3 kg | a house cat |

The tail is about half the body length. Adults are slimmer than the weights suggest, with a body depth only a little more than the head's length.

## Range, habitat and season

**Range.** Rocky fjords and outer skerries from 60 degrees to 68 degrees north on the western coast, below about 300 m of altitude. It is not recorded inland of the first ridge, or south of the last fjord with a sill (underwater ridge) at its mouth.

**Habitat.** North-facing shingle beaches backed by cliff, sea caves with a dry back chamber, and the lee side of skerries. It avoids sandy shores and open water. Roosts need a dry ledge above storm tide, with a view down the fjord.

**Season.**

| Months | Where it is found |
|---|---|
| October to March | in torpor, in a single sea cave or rock crevice, rarely seen |
| April to May | on south-facing rock in the morning sun, warming up |
| June to August | on the outer skerries and shingle, feeding |
| September | moving back toward the winter cave, often along the same shoreline |

Roost sites are reused for decades. Some have been recorded since the 17th century.

## Diet

Seals first, in all months of activity. Also seabird colonies (eggs and sitting adults), stranded whale carcasses, and farm livestock on shore pasture. Prey is taken from the water's edge, in a short rush from a still posture, and is dragged above the tideline to be eaten. Sealers report an adult takes about one seal a week in summer and nothing for weeks after a whale carcass.

**The hoard.** There is gold at some roosts, and there is also a bone pile, driftwood, the iron of a wrecked boat and shed skins. The mixed pile is better described as a nest lining and a midden than as a treasure. Gold appears only where wrecks or burials have put it within reach.

## Activity

Basks on warm rock in the morning, hunts in late afternoon and at dusk, and spends the night in the roost. It needs sun to start moving: a dragon found on cold, wet rock before mid-morning is slow and easily approached (do not approach it). In winter torpor, body temperature falls to near that of the rock, and animals have been found in a state that looks like death.

## Life history

| Stage | What to check |
|---|---|
| Egg | 6 to 10 in a pit among warm shingle, near a steam vent or south-facing slope, laid in June. About the size of a bucket. |
| Hatchling (September) | 0.7 m. Fully banded tail from the start. Climbs and hides among boulders. Easily mistaken for a large lizard. |
| Juvenile (1 to 6 years) | 1 to 4 m. Wings small and unusable. Pale underside more yellow. |
| Subadult (7 to 15 years) | 5 to 8 m. First flights, short, downhill only. Throat flash is small. |
| Adult (15+) | 9 m and above. Length is the clearest age cue. |

Lifespan is unknown, but at least 60 years on the evidence of reused roosts. Eggs take about seven months to hatch.

## Defence and display

The sequence runs in this order, and each stage is something you can see.

1. **Bluff.** The mouth opens wide, the throat flash spreads, and the wings are half-lifted. The animal looks about twice its size for a few seconds. This is a display, and most encounters end here with the animal retreating.
2. **Escape.** It runs upright on its hind legs for 20 to 30 m, then slides into the water. Some individuals bring up a half-digested meal while running, which seems to lighten them.
3. **Fire, if it is used.** Reported at close range only, as a short jet of hot vapour and burning oil, about 5 m long. It is a last measure and is used far less often than the folk accounts say. The scorched lines described above are mostly marks of travel and feeding, and a great many were made by people.
4. **Bite.** Kept for a cornered animal. Wounds are deep and slow to heal.

## Lookalikes

| Species | Separating mark, visible at a distance |
|---|---|
| **Fjord monitor** (large lizard, up to 3 m) | Forked tongue flicking in and out. The fjord wyrm's tongue is not forked and is rarely shown. The monitor is also always on land and always lower to the ground. |
| **Rib-glider** (small gliding lizard, 30 cm) | Wing is a membrane held by 5 to 7 ribs, with the forelimbs on its leading edge. On the wyrm the wing is carried on the arm itself. The glider is also a tree animal. |
| **Two-legged cliff wyvern** | Two legs and wings, where the fjord wyrm has four legs and wings. Count the limbs when the animal is on the ground. |

## Notes

- **Wingless variants.** About one animal in eight in the northern part of the range has no wings at all. They are otherwise identical, including the banded tail. Whether this is a separate form is unsettled. The older writers called them *lindworms*.
- **The name.** *fafnirensis* comes from the saga serpent, and was chosen by the first describer on the strength of the hoard, which later work has not supported.
- **First record.** A parish register entry of 1611 describes a "long beast that lay in the cove below the church, and was seen to eat a seal." The record gives no length.
- **Unresolved.** The fire reports have not been confirmed by any trained observer. If you see one, record the date, the distance and the wind direction.
- **Where to look next.** The cove at the mouth of the second fjord has had a fresh shed skin each August for six years.
