# Cinder-ridge Dragon
***Ignivaranus lindensis*** Voss, 1611

**Scope.** One species, one range. The wide-winged "Alpine" forms and the two-legged "wyverns" of the western valleys are treated separately (see Lookalikes).

**Classification.** Class Reptilia (informal) > Squamata (informal) > Draconidae, fam. nov. > *Ignivaranus*. Four legs plus a separate pair of wings: six limbs in all, which is the first count to check.

**Etymology.** *Igni-* (L. "fire"), for the blackened jaw; *-varanus* (from Ar. *waran*, the monitor lizards), for the body plan; *lindensis*, for the Linden Karst, the place of first record. The common name comes from the row of ridge scutes down the back, which are ash-grey with ember-orange tips.

**First record.** Brother Anselm Voss, 1611, Linden Karst, a sheep-fold ledger entry: "one ewe taken, the track forked at the heel." Voss gave no length. The type specimen is a shed skin, not an animal, so every size below comes from sightings and from the skin.

**Synonyms.** *Draco lindensis* Voss, 1611 (genus preoccupied by the gliding lizards). Lindworm (local, applied loosely to any long reptile in the district). Not the vouivre of the western valleys, which has a different head and no ridge.

---

### Identification
Check these in the order given. The first three can be read at 200 m.

- **Limb count.** Four legs and two wings, six limbs. A two-legged winged form is a wyvern-type, not this animal.
- **Ridge.** A single row of 14 to 17 scutes from the nape to the tail base, each tipped orange. Adults over 60 years show the tips worn flat.
- **Wing.** The membrane is held out by 6 thin ribs. The forelimbs are free and are used for climbing, as in the flying lizards of genus *Draco* (5 to 7 ribs). The wing folds flat against the flank, and a perched animal looks like a long grey lizard with a dark stripe along its side.
- **Jaw.** Teeth are serrated, and the scales along the lower jaw are smoke-black from the gular oil (see Display).
- **Tongue.** Forked, flicked 2 or 3 times a second while walking.

### Size
Total length 7 to 9 m, about the length of a city bus. The body is one-third of that and the tail is half. Wingspan is 1.8 times body length, which is wide enough to span a two-lane road. A frill-like fold of skin behind the head opens to about 1 m, against a skull 70 cm long. Weight is 400 to 600 kg, so a large adult weighs about as much as a bull. Hatchlings are 50 cm long and weigh less than 1 kg, small enough to hold in two hands.

### Range and habitat
Limestone uplands between 900 and 1,600 m, north slope only, from the Linden Karst east to the Tatzel Gorge. Present year-round at the lower end of that band. In winter, adults go down into sinkholes and cave mouths on the south-facing edge of the plateau, where the rock holds heat. A single animal holds about 40 km² of range and uses a group of 3 or 4 caves in rotation. Sightings above 1,800 m or on the lowland plain are out of range and should be doubted until a trace is found.

### Diet
Mostly carrion, found by smell. The tongue samples the air and the animal can locate a carcass at 4 km. It also ambushes livestock and deer on game trails, usually at dawn. A large meal can reach 80 percent of its body weight, after which it may not feed again for several weeks. It does not hoard. Coins and metalwork found in dens are almost always from earlier visitors and not collected by the animal.

### Activity
Active at dawn and again at dusk. It basks at midday on bare rock and flies mainly on thermals, not by flapping, and only for short distances. It flies on warm afternoons, so a sighting in the air at midnight or in a hard frost is unlikely.

### Lifecycle
| Stage | Size | Marker |
|---|---|---|
| Egg | 30 cm, leathery | 12 to 30 per nest, in a mound of leaf litter and ash on a south slope. Incubation lasts 6 to 9 months, so a mound seen in spring dates to the previous autumn. |
| Hatchling | 50 cm | Pale grey, no orange on the ridge. Climbs and hides in scree. |
| Juvenile | 1 to 3 m | Ridge tips appear at about year 3. Wings are present but too small to carry the body. |
| Subadult | 3 to 6 m | Gliding only. First flight at about year 10. |
| Adult | 7 to 9 m | Full size at about 25 years. |
| Old animal | as above | Tips worn flat, jaw black to the chin, slow to leave a basking site. Lifespan 25 to 30 years in the wild by the best estimate. |

### Traces and sightings
- **Track.** A 4-toed print up to 40 cm long, with a drag line from the tail between the prints. The heel print is forked, which is the mark Voss recorded.
- **Shed skin.** Found in whole sheets at cave mouths in late summer. The ridge row can be counted on it.
- **Burnt line.** A narrow strip of scorched grass a few metres long, not a burnt patch. Older records call this "breath". It is a small amount of oil, released from the jaw as a display and lit by the heat of the rock. It is **not** a hunting weapon, and burn marks on a carcass should be treated as post-mortem.
- **Call.** A rasping hiss at close range. At a distance there is a low sound like a stone dragged over stone. There is a smell of hot iron and sulphur close to a den.

### Display and defence
When alarmed it opens its mouth wide, spreads the neck fold and shows the orange throat, much like the frilled lizard of northern Australia (frill up to 30 cm). This is a bluff, and most animals leave after it. If cornered the bite is deep and slow to heal. The animal prefers to run, bipedally over short distances, and take off from a ledge. **Watch out** at a den mouth in late winter, when a female guards eggs, and at a fresh carcass, which it will return to.

### Lookalikes
- **Large monitor lizards** (the Komodo dragon is the usual comparison): no wings and no ridge, but also a forked tongue. Wing absence separates them at once.
- **Gliding lizards, *Draco*:** the same rib-supported membrane, but under 25 cm long.
- **Wyvern-type (two legs, two wings):** the western valley form. Count the legs.
- **Vouivre:** serpentine, no legs, and a gem on the brow in the local accounts.

### Notes and sources
Voss's 1611 ledger and the type skin (Linden Abbey collection) are the only primary records. Body length is taken from tracks and the skin, and the 9 m figure comes from a single sighting, the shepherd Marta Hollin, in 1874 near the Tatzel Gorge. She gave the length by pacing out the shadow, and the figure has been disputed since. The 7 m minimum is more secure. The weight is an estimate scaled from the Komodo dragon and is not a weighing. The diet, hearing and lifecycle figures are borrowed from living analogs (Komodo dragon, flying lizards, frilled lizard) and have not been observed in this species. Whether the animal can actually breathe fire, as the older lore says, is unsettled. The burn lines are real and so is the jaw staining, but no one has yet watched the oil ignite.

**Last recorded:** a fresh track at 1,340 m on the north slope above Linden, 14 September, found by a surveyor who did not see the animal. The next survey should check the south-side sinkholes in February.
