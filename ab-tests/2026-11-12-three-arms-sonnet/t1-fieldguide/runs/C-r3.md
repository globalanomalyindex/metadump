Here is a field guide entry for a dragon, written as a scholar's file from inside the world: a dated record with a named witness, a disputed size and an admitted gap at the end.

---

# Rain-wyrm, *Draco pluvialis*

**Entered** in the Survey of the Tessing Uplands, third register, by Ilse Varhoven, Reader in Reptiles. Revised after the autumn count.
**Scope:** one species, one range. The coastal "sea-wyrm" is kept on a separate sheet. I am not yet convinced it is a separate animal (see Gaps).
**Also called:** river-drake (valley farmers); *lung* (the lowland translators, who borrow it from the eastern word for a weather-serpent).

## Identification
- **Body:** long and low, with no wings. Roughly one-third of its length is neck and one-third is tail. It is carried in loose S-curves, not held upright.
- **Primary mark:** a **pale pearl-grey patch, 12 to 15 cm across, under the chin**. It is visible at 80 m when the animal lifts its head to drink. No monitor lizard has one.
- **Second mark:** **four claws on the hind foot and five on the fore**. Count them on a track in mud (see Traces).
- **Skin:** slate-green on the back, cream below. Bone plates (osteoderms) run in two rows along the spine and darken with age. Old animals are nearly black along the ridge.
- **Crest:** a low rib-supported frill on the neck, folded flat at rest. When flared in threat it roughly doubles the apparent width of the head, as in the frilled-neck lizard of the southern deserts.
- **Teeth:** serrated, curved backward, about 4 cm. The bite does not kill outright. The wound keeps bleeding, as a Komodo's does.

## Size
Adults are about **6 m** from snout to tail tip and weigh about **300 kg**. The figure is disputed. See the witness below.

For scale, the largest verified Komodo monitor was 3.13 m and 166 kg. A rain-wyrm is about twice that length and nearly twice that mass, so it is a **heavy animal** (similar to a bull moose), not a titanic one. A trailing adult leaves a belly-drag trough about 40 cm wide.

## Witness and the disputed size
The only measured adult is the one **Tobiah Hask**, ferryman at Grey Weir, reported on 14 October. He says he laid a 5-m punting pole along the animal where it lay dead on the bank and it was "a hand longer, twice over." That puts it near 5.6 m before the tail, which was cut. I have not seen the carcass. It was buried by the parish before I arrived.

Hask is a careful man with the river and a poor one with numbers. His measure in the register is **6.1 m**. The vicar's copy says **7 m**. I use 6 m as a working figure and expect it to be revised down.

## Range, habitat, season
- **Range:** the Tessing Uplands only, in the valleys between **Harrow Fell and the Sallow Ridge**. Records run from the 400-m contour up to about 900 m, never above the tree line.
- **Habitat:** north-facing scree above peat bog and cold, slow rivers. Dens are in undercut banks and flooded limestone sinks.
- **Season:** seen at the water from **March to May** and again in **October**. In the dry months it keeps to the dens. After heavy rain it is seen more often, and on days with low cloud.

## Diet and hunting
It feeds on red deer, sheep, otters and large fish. It ambushes from still water and drags the prey under. It eats a carcass over several days, as crocodiles do. Hunting is mostly at **dusk and before dawn**, and it basks at midday on warm stones. It eats little in winter.

## Life cycle
Clutches of **about 20 leathery eggs** are laid in June, in a hollow in river gravel. Incubation takes about nine months, so hatchlings appear in the following March. Hatchlings are about 40 cm long and slate-coloured, and they climb well. Dens hold young of two seasons together. The sex of the young seems to depend on the **temperature of the nest**, as it does in alligators. Wardens report that cold nests have yielded mostly males. The count is small and I do not rely on it. Adults live to perhaps 40 years.

## Traces and sightings
- **Track:** five-toed forefoot and four-toed hindfoot, 22 cm long, with a drag groove between. The tail marks are intermittent.
- **Shed skin:** found in alder roots in spring, in sheets up to 2 m. It has a faint pearl sheen on the throat piece.
- **Pellets:** rolls of bone, hair and fish scale on flat stones.
- **Weather:** farmers say the wyrm "calls the rain." I record that the animal is most active just before storms, when prey is on the move. I do not record that it causes them.

## Cautions
The bite is the danger, and the infection after it. Keep to the high bank when you are near the water in spring. The flared frill is a warning. When it opens, withdraw and do not run along the bank. The animal is much faster in water than on land.

## Lookalikes
- **Giant water monitor:** also long and dark, but with a forked yellow tongue, no chin pearl and five claws on every foot.
- **Eel-snake (misnamed):** no legs.

## Gaps
I have not seen the animal alive, only its sign, and I have not seen it breathe. The older registers say it "exhales mist." That could be a cold-morning breath, and I have written nothing about it. The sea-wyrm sheet will say whether the pearl is present on the coastal animals. For now I have only the word of a ferryman.
