Mammoth Steak: a bone-in bison rib steak, reverse-seared and carved at the table

What "mammoth" means here. Nobody can sell you real woolly mammoth. The permafrost carcasses are intact, but the record shows dogs and wild animals eating them, and one researcher who fried old meat got a strange liquid. A lab-grown mammoth meatball exists, but it was grown in a sheep cell and nobody has tasted it. So this recipe takes both senses of the word. The animal is bison, the closest buyable big game cut. The size is huge: a tall, bone-in rib steak about 2 inches thick, served whole on a slate or board. Bison is the mildest of the common game meats.

Reindeer fillet is a smaller, leaner alternative. Swedish online game butchers sell it frozen. Elephant is a bad idea. In the US, parts other than ivory need federal permits (50 CFR parts 13, 14 and 23), and I couldn't confirm the meat rules.

Serves 4 at the table, plus a second meal from the leftovers.
Time: 1 day of salting, about 2.5 hours on the day (mostly hands-off).

Buy

  • One bone-in bison ribeye (a "tomahawk" or "cowboy" cut), 1.4 kg / 3 lb, 5 cm / 2 in thick. Ranches ship bison online across the US, and some Canadian retailers sell flash-frozen packs.
  • Portions are counted by weight, not by steak. About 1 kg of meat comes off the bone, which makes four 250 g servings and leaves some over.

If it arrives frozen, thaw it first. Put it in the fridge at 4 C / 40 F or below for about 24 hours per 2 kg (4 to 5 lb). This cut needs about 1.5 days, so start two days before you want to salt it. Thawing comes before the salting.

Ingredients

For the steak:

  • 1 bone-in bison ribeye, 1.4 kg / 3 lb, thawed if it was frozen
  • 12 g Diamond Crystal kosher salt, about 0.85% of the meat's weight. Weigh it. If you use Morton, use 8 g, because a teaspoon of Morton is about 1.7 times as heavy as Diamond Crystal.
  • 6 g coarsely cracked black pepper (about 2 tsp), added late
  • 2 tbsp (30 ml) high smoke-point oil, such as avocado or refined sunflower

For the finishing butter:

  • 60 g unsalted butter
  • 4 garlic cloves, smashed
  • 2 tbsp chopped rosemary and thyme, mixed
  • 4 juniper berries, lightly crushed (optional, but they give a pine-forest feel)

Method

1. Dry-brine (24 hours ahead, or 12 at minimum). Pat the steak dry with paper towels. Sprinkle the salt over every surface, including the sides, the fat cap and around the bone. Set it on a rack over a tray and leave it uncovered in the fridge. The salt seasons inward and dries the surface, and a dry surface browns instead of steaming grey. A 5 cm cut needs the long end of that window.

2. Warm up (1 hour before cooking). Take the steak out of the fridge. Heat the oven to 110 C / 225 F, with a rack in the middle. Pat the steak dry again if it looks damp. Leave the pepper off for now, since it scorches in a long high sear.

3. Slow roast. Set the steak on its rack and tray in the oven. Insert an oven probe into the centre of the meat, not touching the bone. Roast until the centre reads 48 C / 118 F, which takes about 60 to 90 minutes. Rely on the probe reading, not the clock. A thick cut lags: the centre takes far longer to rise than the surface takes to brown.

4. First rest (10 minutes). Take the steak out and tent it loosely with foil. Meanwhile, put a cast-iron pan over high heat for 5 minutes, until it smokes lightly. Add the oil.

5. Render and sear.

  • Hold the steak edge-on with tongs and render the fat cap for 3 to 4 minutes, until clear and crisp.
  • Press the pepper onto the faces.
  • Sear 45 to 60 seconds per side, turning once, then 15 seconds on each edge.
  • The sear only browns. The oven did the cooking, so a longer sear just widens the grey band.
  • Total sear time is under 4 minutes.

6. Baste. Drop the heat to medium and add the butter, garlic, herbs and juniper. When the butter foams, tilt the pan and spoon it over the steak for 30 to 45 seconds. Garlic goes in this late so it perfumes the butter without burning.

7. Final rest (15 to 20 minutes). Put the steak on a board or slate, loosely tented, with the pan butter spooned over it. The core climbs another 5 to 10 F while it rests. A roast-sized cut needs this long rest. If you carve early, the juices run onto the board.

8. Carve at the table. Cut the meat away from the bone in one piece, then slice across the grain into 6 to 12 mm (1/4 to 1/2 in) slices. Each slice shows a band of brown crust, a thin grey ring and a rosy centre. Return the bone to the platter. It's the best part for people to gnaw on.

Doneness

| Core after the final rest | Result |
|---|---|
| 52 to 54 C / 125 to 130 F | Rare to medium-rare |
| 54 to 57 C / 130 to 135 F | Medium-rare (what the times above aim for) |
| 60 C / 140 F | Medium |

Safety note. Whole-muscle steak is contaminated on the surface, which the sear takes care of. The core only needs to hit your chosen temperature. Official guidance is 63 C / 145 F for intact whole-muscle meat, including farmed game. Some state guidance, such as Penn State's for venison, says 71 C / 160 F. The sources disagree. If you serve elderly, pregnant or immunocompromised guests, go to 63 C (145 F) and expect a drier steak.

If something goes wrong

  • The core is still low after the final rest. Put the steak back on its rack in a 110 C / 225 F oven until the probe reads your target, rest 5 minutes, then give it a quick sear of 20 to 30 seconds a side.
  • There's a wide grey band under the crust. This comes from browning for too long, and I don't have a fix for it. Shorten the sear next time, and warm the centre fully in the oven first. For this meal, slice thin and serve with the butter.
  • The meat tastes gamey. Most of the gamey taste sits in the fat and the surface. Trim silver skin and the outer fat before salting. Or marinate for 1 to 4 hours (juniper, red wine or vinegar), then rinse and dry well before the dry-brine. A long acid soak leaves the surface soft.

Serving the ice-age table

Bring the steak whole to the table on slate or a wooden board, bone up, with the herbs piled around it like something dug out of the tundra. Serve with:

  • Roasted root vegetables: parsnip, celeriac and beet, cooked at 200 C (400 F) for 40 minutes
  • Dark rye bread
  • Lingonberry or cranberry sauce
  • A coarse salt dish for the table

Leftovers. Refrigerate within 2 hours. Slice cold into thin strips for a second meal: steak sandwiches, or a hash with the root vegetables and a fried egg.

Variations

  • Beef version. A bone-in beef ribeye or tomahawk, same weight and thickness, works with the same method.
  • Smaller cut. Scale the time down, because the centre lags less. A 3 cm / 1.25 in steak reaches 48 C (118 F) in about 25 to 35 minutes, then sears 60 to 90 seconds a side.
  • Salt and pepper only. Skip the juniper, herbs and garlic, and the meat's own taste stays most present.
